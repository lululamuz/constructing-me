CONSTRUCTING ME

A systems-based self portrait

This project started from Stuart Hall’s idea that identity is not something fixed that we simply discover about ourselves, but something that is continuously formed through representation, culture, and the way we are seen. His idea of identity as a process of “becoming” made me think about how many different systems are involved in constructing a version of who I am. I began by looking at how I represent myself, then how Google turns my behavior into data and categories, and finally how familiar identities from media can be applied back onto a person. Rather than trying to create one accurate self-portrait, Constructing Me looks at the different processes that keep producing and reshaping a sense of self.

ME, ACCORDING TO ME

Self-perception and control

This project started from a 5 minute mirror meditation exercise. I was asked to look into my own eyes in silence for five minutes. At first, I could focus on my eyes, but after a while my attention started drifting to other parts of my face, to my skin, hair, acne marks, and individual features. I started thinking about what I would change and whether certain features would look better if they were different. When I tried to bring my attention back to my eyes, I noticed the redness in them and immediately started wondering if I had not been resting enough.

That experience made me realize how difficult it is for me to simply “look” at myself without analyzing or correcting what I see. I wanted to continue that idea through drawing.

I drew myself three times, each time with a different level of control. For the first drawing, I could not look at the paper or lift my pen. For the second, I still could not look at the paper, but I was allowed to stop and start a new line. For the third, I could look back and forth between the mirror and the drawing.

What interested me was how different the drawings became as I was given more control. Once I could actually see what I was drawing, I also started correcting it and making more decisions about how I wanted myself to look. It made me realize that looking at myself is never completely neutral. The more control I had, the more the portrait became not just what I saw, but how I chose to represent what I saw.

ME, ACCORDING TO DATA

This project came from looking through the profile Google’s My Ad Center had built about me. It had assigned me ten different identity tags based on my activity and data. Some were accurate, some were clearly wrong, and some were things Google did not seem fully sure about.

I separated the tags into three groups: the ones it got right, the ones it got wrong, and all of them combined. I then used each group as the prompt for an ai generated portrait.

I was interested in what happens when a person is reduced to a list of categories. Even when some of the information was inaccurate, the system could still produce a version of “me.” The portraits made me think about how data platforms do not need to know me completely in order to construct an identity for me. They only need enough information to classify, predict, and make assumptions about who I might be.

ME, ACCORDING TO TAGS

Identity templates I have learned to recognize

This project goes back to something I remember from taking Media Studies in high school. We talked about how media has shaped the identities people are encouraged to take on at different points in time. The examples that stayed with me most were the contrast between images like Rosie the Riveter, which encouraged women to enter the workforce during wartime, and older household advertisements that presented domestic work as part of being a desirable or “good” woman.

The images have changed, but I do not think the process has disappeared. Today, ideas of what it means to be attractive, successful, productive, feminine, healthy, or effortless are constantly repeated through advertising, social media, film, influencers, and fashion. After seeing these representations again and again, we learn how these identities are supposed to look, and sometimes begin using them to build or adjust our own image.

For this project, I turned several of these familiar identities into draggable visual templates. Each tag changes the same avatar based on the visual language I have learned to associate with that identity.

The question I kept coming back to was: if we learn so many of the pieces from media, where does our identity actually come from?

IDENTITY TEMPLATES

These tags are not supposed to define who someone really is. They are familiar identities that media and culture have repeated so often that we already know what they are “supposed” to look like.
